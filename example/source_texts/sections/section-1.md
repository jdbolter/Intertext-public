## A Map Is a Choice

A map of a city is made by deciding what to leave out. A street plan shows roads and drops the trees; a transit diagram shows stations and drops the streets; a tourist map shows the cathedral at twice its size and the car park not at all. None of these maps is wrong, and each answers a question that someone put to the city before drawing began.

Harry Beck’s diagram of the London Underground, first published in 1933, is the famous case. Beck straightened the lines, spaced the stations evenly and discarded the true distances between them, because a traveller underground needs to know where to change trains and not how far apart the stations lie. The diagram was an argument that connection matters more than distance for this reader, and it was persuasive enough that transit systems around the world have imitated it.

So the first thing to ask of any map is what it was made to help someone do. The same stretch of coast drawn for a sailor, a hiker and a developer will differ in what it shows and in what it lets the eye pass over, and the differences are the map’s reasoning made visible.
