## Borders That Are Not There

A border on a map is a line; on the ground it is, much of the time, nothing at all. A stretch of open country may show no sign that one state ends and another begins, and the line exists as an agreement recorded on paper and kept by the people who have reason to keep it.

Where a border follows a river or a ridge, it borrows a feature the land already has. Where it follows a parallel of latitude or a straight edge between two points, it was drawn first and the country arranged around it. Maps of the second kind make the clearest case that a line can be an act of decision, performed by whoever holds the pencil and can make others abide by it.

J. B. Harley urged readers of maps to look for the social forces that shaped them and for the presence of power in what they show (Harley 1989, 2). His point was that a map seldom says of itself that it is an instrument of someone’s interests, and that the calm of its surface is part of how it works.
